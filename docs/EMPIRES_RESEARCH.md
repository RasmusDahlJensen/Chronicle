## 1. What history shows

### How empires rose
- **A cohesive core with an edge.**
  - Groups held together by kinship and shared enemies (Ibn Khaldun's *asabiyyah*) conquered settled states: the Mongols, the Achaemenids, the early Ottomans, the Romans of the Republic.
  - Large empires arose disproportionately where steppe meets farmland: over 90% of more than 60 historical mega-empires lay in or beside the arid belt from the Sahara to the Gobi (Turchin 2009). Nomads and farmers forced each other to scale up.
- **A military edge that later spread:** chariots, mounted archers, Roman legions, the British navy, the Moroccan muskets that beat Songhai at Tondibi (1591). Conquests came in waves after a breakthrough, until rivals caught up.
- **Conquest that paid for itself.**
  - Loot and tribute funded the next campaign (Rome, the Mongols).
  - The Inca had to conquer: each new ruler inherited no estates, because a dead ruler's lands stayed with his lineage.
  - When the frontier reached poor or strong land, the income stopped but the army's cost did not.
- **Turning the conquered into strength:**
  - Rome bound defeated Italians as allies who owed troops;
  - Genghis broke up tribal lineages into decimal army units and recruited the engineers of the peoples he conquered;
  - the Ottomans raised Christian boys as soldiers and officials (*devşirme*);
  - the British ruled through local princes and chiefs.
- **Money and credit.** Britain after 1688 could tax heavily and borrow cheaply, and spent over 60% of its budget on war (Brewer's "fiscal-military state"). American silver let Spain fight everywhere, until it could not.
- **Luck:** a run of wet years fed the Mongols' horses during their rise (Pederson et al. 2014); epidemics broke the Americas before the Spanish.

### How they held what they took
Roads and relay posts: Roman roads, the Persian royal road, the Mongol *yam*, 40,000 km of Inca roads. Governors watched by inspectors: Persian satraps and the "king's eyes", Charlemagne's counts and *missi*. Tolerance of local religion and law (Persia, the Mongols), or forced resettlement of suspect peoples (the Inca). A shared identity offered to the conquered: Roman citizenship, China's examinations. Garrisons on the frontier.

### How they fell or shrank
- **Succession.** Almost every Mongol succession was contested, and after 1260 the empire ran as four khanates. Alexander died without an adult heir, and his generals fought for 40 years. Frankish custom divided Charlemagne's realm among his sons. The Inca brothers' civil war opened the way for Pizarro.
- **The army making rulers:**
  - Rome's crisis of 235–284 saw more than 20 emperors in 50 years.
  - In the Abbasid "anarchy at Samarra" (861–870), Turkish slave-soldiers made and unmade caliphs.
  - Janissaries deposed Ottoman sultans.
- **Fiscal crisis:**
  - Rome's silver coin fell from about 50% silver to about 4% (c. 258–270);
  - Philip II suspended payments four times;
  - pressing Catalonia and Portugal to share Spain's costs set off their revolts in 1640.
- **Governors becoming princes.**
  - Abbasid governors founded hereditary dynasties while the caliph survived as a symbol.
  - Carolingian counts became hereditary lords during the Viking raids.
  - Mali's outer provinces broke away under weak successors.
- **Elites outgrowing the positions:** long prosperity bred more nobles and would-be officials than there were posts, and they fought for them (England, France, Russia, Rome; Turchin & Nefedov).
- **A shock at the centre on an already strained realm:**
  - Spanish America formed its own governments when Napoleon captured the king (1808) and never returned;
  - the USSR's republics left within months of the failed 1991 coup;
  - the Ilkhanate disintegrated after an heirless death in a plague year (1335).
- **Plague and climate:** Rome's pandemics of 165, the 250s and 541, and late-antique cooling; dry years drove nomads into China (Bai & Kung 2011).
- **Nationalism:** from the 19th century, cultural difference turned into independence movements: the Ottoman Balkans, 1918, decolonization after 1945, 1991. Britain's retreat was mostly managed, after two world wars drained the treasury.

## 2. Theories, and how well they hold up

| Theory | Claim | Standing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ibn Khaldun's *asabiyyah* | Cohesive groups from the margins conquer; luxury erodes their cohesion in about three generations | Insightful, untested |
| Turchin's metaethnic frontiers | Cohesion forms where very different peoples meet in conflict, and fades in a safe heartland | Moderately supported; a model built on that frontier and on military tech spreading from the steppe explains 65% of where large states arose (Turchin et al. 2013) |
| Secular cycles (Goldstone; Turchin & Nefedov) | Population growth, then elite overproduction and fiscal distress, then breakdown; cycles of 200–300 years with about 50-year waves of violence | Well supported in case studies; a database of 168 crises finds elite overproduction almost everywhere, outcomes varied |
| Tainter | Complexity solves problems at falling returns until upkeep eats the surplus, and a shock makes collapse the cheaper option | Influential, hard to test |
| Kennedy's imperial overstretch | Commitments outgrow the economic base while rivals catch up | Good pattern (Spain, Britain) |
| Glubb's *Fate of Empires* (about 250 years each) | Empires pass through fixed ages | Weak: cherry-picked, not used in scholarship |
| Dynastic cycle, Mandate of Heaven | Rule lasts while it delivers; disaster and tyranny lose it | Formal models support the mechanism (Usher 1989) |
| Olson's stationary bandit | A secure ruler taxes moderately and keeps order; an insecure one plunders. Long stability breeds privileged coalitions and sclerosis | Well established |
| Acemoglu & Robinson | Inclusive institutions give lasting growth, extractive ones stall | Broad support; elastic categories |

**Lifespans:**
- Arbesman (2011), on Taagepera's 41 ancient empires, finds lifespans fit a constant yearly risk of collapse with a mean of about 220 years. Age alone does not doom an empire: about 10% last past 500 years and 1% past 1,000.
- Taagepera & Nemčok (2024), on 98 empires: the top empires stayed at half their peak size or more for about 180 years, with no lengthening over five millennia even as peak sizes grew. Decline took about 2.5 times as long as the rise, and slow growth gave more durable states.

## 3. Rulers and succession

- **Kinds of rulers:**
  - conquerors (Cyrus, Alexander, Genghis, Charlemagne);
  - consolidators (Darius I, Augustus, Ögedei);
  - restorers (Diocletian) and reformers whose reforms unravelled the state (Gorbachev);
  - weak, child and regency rulers: 8 of the 14 Eastern Han emperors took the throne as boys;
  - long, steady reigns (Kangxi, 61 years).
- **Rulers matter where nothing constrains them.**
  - When leaders died in office, growth shifted significantly, mainly in autocracies (Jones & Olken 2005).
  - Abler European monarchs changed borders and state performance, but not where parliaments constrained them (Ottinger & Voigtländer).
  - Land, money, elites and geography set the trend; rulers set its timing and direction, most of all in crises.
- **Succession type decides stability.** European monarchs, 1000–1800 (Kokkonen & Sundell 2014):

| Succession | Monarchs | Deposed | Mean reign | Yearly risk of deposition, roughly |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Eldest son (primogeniture) | 451 | 16% | 21.6 years | 0.7% |
| Elective | 386 | 49% | 12.4 years | 4% |
| Brothers and kin first | 124 | 57% | 9.1 years | 6% |

- **Violence:**
  - about 22% of 1,513 European monarchs (600–1800) died violently and 15% were murdered, with murders falling sharply over the centuries (Eisner 2011);
  - 62% of Roman emperors died violently, the risk highest in a reign's first year and rising again after about 12 years (Saleh 2019);
  - a comparison read only through a summary finds Chinese emperors at about half the Roman rate;
  - Chinese reigns fit a constant risk with a half-life of about 10 years.
- **Other systems:**
  - **Contested kin succession** (the Mongols, the early Ottomans) brought bloody contests that sometimes produced strong winners.
  - **Partible inheritance** split realms among sons, often with civil war (the Carolingians, Kievan Rus).
  - **Elective monarchy** invited foreign meddling (Poland-Lithuania, partitioned 1772–95).
  - **Modern dictatorships:** about half of coup attempts succeed; most dictators who fall are removed by insiders; military regimes are shortest-lived and one-party states longest.
  - **Democracies** change leaders on a schedule, and policy shifts without a change of regime.

## 4. What this means for Chronicle

Chronicle already has the slow pressures. M3c added:
- administration costs that grow with size, distance and age;
- taxes bounded by how calm the people are;
- arrears, deferred maintenance and visible neglect;
- famine relief that rich realms can afford;
- strain on far provinces.

The research says strain alone should mostly cost output and make an empire's edges brittle, as it does now. The big losses come when a **shock hits the centre** of a strained realm: a disputed succession, a ruler who dies with no fit heir, a lost war, a default, a plague. Chronicle has no shocks at the centre yet, so its empires slowly grind instead of breaking.

Simple rules that would bring the historical patterns in (graded, never scripted):

1. **Rulers with traits, ages and natural deaths.** Each civilization has a ruler with one or two traits: Conqueror, Builder, Reformer, Cautious, Greedy, Cruel, Pious, Diplomatic, Weak. Traits shift how they tax, build, keep up the realm, expand, unite and (from M6) go to war. Their weight grows with how unconstrained the government is. Rulers age and die along a human mortality curve.
2. **Succession by government form:**
   - chiefdoms pass power among kin, and contests are frequent;
   - hereditary monarchies pass it to the eldest son;
   - elective monarchies let the great families choose;
   - some cultures divide the realm among sons;
   - later, republics and democracies hold elections, dictatorships end in coups or appointed heirs, and one-party states let the party choose.
   - Starting values for the yearly risk of removal: about 0.7% for primogeniture, 4% for elective monarchy and 6% for kin succession.
   - The risk is highest early in a reign (Saleh), and higher after a predecessor was removed. Parliaments lowered it (Kokkonen & Sundell). Powerful elites and soldiers raised it (Blaydes & Chaney; Powell & Thyne on coups).
3. **Legitimacy by performance** (the Mandate of Heaven): it rises with prosperity, wonders, victories and long reigns, and falls with famine, arrears, neglect, defeat and debasement. Low legitimacy raises the risk of removal and of regions joining a revolt.
4. **A crisis at the centre:** a disputed succession, a child ruler, a removed ruler, the capital lost, a default. While it lasts, every region's chance of breaking away rises. Regions that remember a former state, have another culture, or have drifted far from the capital go first. Breakups then cluster after crises, with long quiet stretches between, matching the constant-risk lifetimes.
5. **Governors drifting away:** regions beyond the capital's reach slowly gain autonomy. They send less tax, become nominal vassals, and leave at the next crisis.
6. **Accumulated privilege instead of a fixed age cost:** the age factor in administration costs grows in peaceful years and is cut back by a Reformer, a new dynasty, a civil war or a conquest.
7. **Restoration:** whoever holds a fallen empire's capital or core people is drawn to reunite its fragments.
8. **Later milestones:** frontier cohesion (M4–M7), a spreading military edge, conquest that pays and then stops (M6), credit and default (from Banking), elites outgrowing their positions (M7), and nationalism from Printing on (M7).

**Health checks to add** (never quotas):
- large realms stay at half their peak size or more for a median of one to two centuries, with a long tail;
- mean reigns by succession type are near 20, 12 and 9 years;
- realms that rose fastest tend to break first;
- breakups cluster after crises at the centre.

**Story beats players would recognise:**
- a conqueror's empire split among warring heirs;
- steppe horsemen uniting under one khan, then drifting into khanates;
- a child king's court hollowing out the throne;
- soldiers making and unmaking emperors;
- debased coin, heavy taxes and provincial revolt;
- far governors becoming hereditary princes;
- plague during a disputed succession;
- distant colonies governing themselves after the capital falls;
- a long-lived empire losing its edges to independence movements;
- a reforming emperor restoring the old empire;
- a dynasty losing the Mandate after famines while the state endures.
